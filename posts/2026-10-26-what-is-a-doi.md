A **DOI** (Digital Object Identifier) is a permanent ID number for a research paper, dataset, book chapter or other scholarly item. Like an ISBN for books, it identifies one item for ever — even if the publisher moves its website.

## What does a DOI look like?

A DOI always starts with **10.**, followed by a code for the registering organisation, a slash, and an ID for the item:

> **10.1038/s41586-020-2649-2**

To open it, put **https://doi.org/** in front:

> https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2649-2

The doi.org address always sends you to the item's current location.

## Why DOIs matter

- **Links never break.** Web addresses change; DOIs are kept up to date by the publisher.
- **Exact references.** A DOI points to one specific paper, so there is no confusion between papers with similar titles.
- **Citation tracking.** Databases such as OpenAlex use DOIs to connect papers and count citations — the numbers you see on ResearchBoard rely on them.

## Who gives out DOIs?

DOIs are managed by the International DOI Foundation and issued through registration agencies. For journal articles the main agency is **Crossref**; for datasets it is often **DataCite**. Journals, preprint servers and data repositories register a DOI when they publish.

## How to find a paper's DOI

- Look at the first page of the paper or the top of its web page.
- Search the title on [Crossref Search](https://search.crossref.org).
- In Google Scholar or PubMed, the DOI usually appears in the full record.

## Using DOIs in your references

Most reference styles (APA, Vancouver, Harvard and others) now ask you to include the DOI as a link at the end of each reference, for example:

> Author, A. (2020). Title of the paper. *Journal Name*, 12(3), 45–67. https://doi.org/10.xxxx/xxxxx

Reference managers such as Zotero or Mendeley can fill in everything automatically from a DOI.
