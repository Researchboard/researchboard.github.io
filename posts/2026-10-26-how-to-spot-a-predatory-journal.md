A **predatory journal** charges authors a fee to publish but skips the real editorial work: no proper peer review, no careful editing and often no long-term archiving. Publishing there can harm your reputation and waste good research. Early-career researchers are the most common targets.

## 12 warning signs

1. **An unexpected email invites you to submit**, full of flattery ("Dear Esteemed Professor") — often about a topic you do not work on.
2. **Very fast acceptance promised**, such as "peer review in 72 hours".
3. **The fee is hidden** or only mentioned after your paper is accepted.
4. **A very broad scope**, such as "International Journal of Science, Engineering and Medicine".
5. **A name that copies a famous journal** with one word changed.
6. **The editorial board is fake or unclear** — names without institutions, people who say they never joined, or no board listed at all.
7. **Fake or misleading metrics**, such as an "impact factor" from an unknown organisation instead of Journal Citation Reports.
8. **Claims of indexing that are not true.** Check yourself whether the journal is really listed in the databases it names.
9. **No clear peer-review process** described on the website.
10. **Poor website quality**: spelling mistakes, broken links, stock photos, or an address that does not match the country it claims.
11. **No retraction or correction policy**, and no information about archiving (for example with CLOCKSS or Portico).
12. **Published papers look weak**: many grammar errors, unrelated topics side by side, or obvious mistakes that review would catch.

One sign alone does not prove a journal is predatory, but several together are a strong warning.

## How to check a journal

- **Think. Check. Submit.** — the free checklist at [thinkchecksubmit.org](https://thinkchecksubmit.org) walks you through the key questions.
- **DOAJ:** quality open-access journals are listed in the [Directory of Open Access Journals](https://doaj.org). Being missing is not proof of a problem, but being listed is a good sign.
- **Check the indexing claims** directly on the database websites rather than trusting the journal's logo images.
- **Ask a senior colleague or your librarian** — they see these journals often.
- **Read a few recent papers** in the journal. Would you be proud to have your work next to them?

## What to do if you already submitted

If you realise the journal is suspicious before publication, write to withdraw your paper in writing and keep a copy of the email. Do not pay any fee you were not clearly told about in advance. Ask your institution's library or research office for help.
